Does care coordination in integrated care reduce unmet healthcare needs among older adults?

CAT Report: Critical Appraisal of a policy Topic
📚 10 studies🏷 A 8 · B 2 🔎 Type1
Qualified Yes (provisional): the direction that service coordination reduces older adults' unmet needs is supported, but the evidence is thin and the effect is domain-specific.
Credibility Weak~ModerateConfidence Low~Moderate10 studies

I. Policy Decision Issue

Policy contextFollowing the 2018 Community Care Master Plan, Korea ran pilot projects in 16 local governments in 2019 and an integrated medical and care support pilot for older adults in 12 local governments in 2023, and in March 2024 enacted the Act on Integrated Support for Community Care including Medical and Long-term Care, with nationwide expansion to all municipalities planned. As of August 2025, 57% of the 229 basic local governments were participating in the pilot [K4]. The core instrument of this policy is coordinating fragmented health, medical, long-term care and welfare services around each recipient (care coordination).
SignificanceUnmet healthcare need is a flagship outcome that integrated care promises to reduce, and it directly reveals delivery failures in which services exist but do not reach recipients. Internationally, 10.0% (95% CI 9.2–10.9) of people aged 65 and over experience unmet healthcare needs and 25.1% (95% CI 17.1–34.2) unmet long-term care needs [Ev08]. Among Korean older adults the rate of unmet healthcare need is 17.4%, and the most common reason was financial hardship (9.2%) [K3]. Unmet healthcare need is known to raise disease severity, complications and mortality risk, so the cost of leaving it unaddressed is high.
Focused uncertaintyThe uncertainty is not whether integrated care is good but whether the instrument of service coordination reduces the specific outcome of unmet healthcare need. Most of the effectiveness literature on integrated care uses hospital admissions, emergency department use, length of stay, costs and quality of life as outcomes. Studies that directly measure unmet needs are rare, and those that do disagree. Moreover, if the main reason for unmet healthcare need among Korean older adults is financial hardship [K3], how far a coordination-centred intervention acts on this pathway is a separate issue.
GoalTo answer, with international A- and B-level evidence, whether service coordination reduces unmet healthcare needs among older adults, and to distinguish the conditions under which effects do and do not appear. On that basis, Korean pilot evaluations are compared separately, only to judge applicability, in order to derive implementation recommendations for the design of nationwide expansion.

Stopped at Q1: meta-analysis of 101 observational studies (cross-sectional and cohort), with NOS quality appraisal and I² heterogeneity review. It synthesizes only observational studies and estimates prevalence rather than intervention effects, hence minus.
Full text P and O match (unmet healthcare and long-term care needs of older people). There is no I: it is not an intervention study but a meta-analysis estimating the size of the problem and the structure of barriers. Table 1 of the article: pooled prevalence of overall unmet healthcare need (type not specified) 10.0% (95% CI 9.2–10.9, 73 studies); medical care 10.4% (95% CI 7.3–13.9, 4 studies). Unmet long-term care need 25.1% (95% CI 17.1–34.2, 13 studies), i.e. 1 in 4 older people. Random-effects model. The abstract gives overall unmet healthcare need as 10.4%, which differs from the table. If the main barriers to meeting needs are affordability and geographic access, part of the problem lies beyond the reach of coordination alone. Cost relief and transport support must go together with it.

Abstract P (frail older people), I (coordinated and integrated interventions) and C match. O centres on healthcare utilization, not unmet healthcare need. Full text not obtained. N/A. Stopped at Q1: systematic review of 10 RCTs and 5 comparative observational studies with independent quality appraisal by 2 reviewers. Mainly experimental, but no meta-analysis because of heterogeneity and no test of publication bias, hence 0.
Full text P (users of community aged care), I (case management), C and Cx match. O directly includes unmet service needs. N/A. The authors state that, because of heterogeneity in study designs, participants, interventions and outcome measures, they did not conduct a meta-analysis but a narrative synthesis following PRISMA. All 3 studies that measured unmet service needs consistently reported fewer unmet needs. Psychological health and well-being improved significantly in 5 of 7 studies. Evidence on other client and carer outcomes was limited. The conclusion is that case management in community aged care can improve clients' psychological health and well-being and their unmet service needs. This is the evidence that answers this CAT question most directly. However, it rests on only 3 studies with no pooled estimate, so the size of the effect cannot be stated. VI. Evidence Synthesis: Conclusion

CAT ConclusionQualified Yes (provisional): the direction that service coordination reduces older adults' unmet needs is supported, but the evidence is thin and the effect is domain-specific.
Key findingsEvidence that directly measured unmet needs falls into two strands. In a systematic review of case management in community aged care, all 3 studies that measured it consistently reported fewer unmet service needs [Ev10]. By contrast, an RCT of home-based care coordination for older adults with memory disorders found no significant group difference in total unmet needs, with significant reductions only in the safety and legal/advance care domains [Ev06]. On the adjacent outcome of access to care, there is evidence that integrated care improves access [Ev01]. On utilization indicators, significant reductions such as unnecessary admissions of 4.1% vs. 11.7% (p=.03) are reported [Ev07], but overall results are inconsistent [Ev03] and most reported outcomes of preventive integrated care showed no effect [Ev04].
Boundary conditions① Effects do not appear immediately: in a multidisciplinary home-visit intervention the hazard ratio for admission was not significant in year 1 at 0.94 (p=.71) and became significant at 0.48 (95% CI 0.24–0.95, p=.04) in years 2–3 [Ev07]. ② Effects are domain-specific: they appeared in specific domains such as safety and advance care rather than in the total [Ev06]. ③ The narrower the target group, the clearer the evidence: effects were measured in groups with complex needs such as frail and dependent older people and older adults with memory disorders [Ev06][Ev07][Ev09]. ④ Some barriers lie beyond the reach of coordination: the main barriers to meeting needs are affordability and geographic access [Ev08], and demand-side attitudes (preference for family care, distrust of the system, stigma) also constrain access [Ev05].
Conflicting / limitsThe two most direct pieces of evidence point in different directions: the case management review consistently reports fewer unmet needs [Ev10], while the only RCT reports no effect on total unmet needs [Ev06]. Neither provides a pooled effect size (both skipped meta-analysis because of heterogeneity or found significance only in sub-domains). Moreover, studies measuring unmet healthcare need as an outcome are themselves rare, so there is no basis for reading positive results on utilization indicators as reductions in unmet needs.

IX. Policy Recommendation & Action Plan

Short-term (6–12 months)① Add unmet healthcare and care needs to performance indicators by domain (safety, medication, mobility, advance care, etc.). Total indicators alone cannot capture the domain-specific effect shown by [Ev06]. ② Build a baseline by calculating unmet-need indicators retrospectively from existing pilot data. ③ Prioritize groups with complex needs (frail, dependent, cognitively impaired) as coordination recipients: these are the groups in which effects were found [Ev06][Ev07][Ev09]. ④ Unmet needs due to financial reasons are not resolved by coordination [Ev08][K3], so design cost relief and transport support into the coordination package.
Mid/Long-term (2–3 years)① Set the evaluation point at least 2 years out; a 1-year evaluation may miss the effect [Ev07]. ② Keep comparison groups in the phased expansion across local governments so that quasi-experimental evaluation is possible (Korean pilot evaluations have already been carried out this way [K1][K2]). ③ Organizational and financial integration (meso and macro levels) is an evidence gap [Ev02], so run the expansion itself as a learning design that generates its own evidence. ④ Run efforts to improve demand-side awareness and trust as a separate program in parallel [Ev05].
ConfidenceLow~Moderate. The direction (reduction in unmet needs) is supported, but the size cannot be stated; only 2 studies match the outcome construct and they disagree. Rather than justifying full implementation, the evidence supports recommending phased expansion with indicators and observation over 2 years or more.
Consideration (potential harms)① Stronger coordination may increase readmissions: in an evaluation of post-discharge integrated care in Korea, the hazard ratio for readmission with the same diagnosis was 3.53 (95% CI 2.98–4.19), higher than in the control group [K1]. This may reflect needs being found and linked to care, but unintended increases in use cannot be ruled out, so monitoring is needed. ② Managing by total indicators may create a selection effect in which easy domains are filled while hard ones (financial reasons, severe cases) remain. ③ More assessment and planning procedures may shift administrative burden onto recipients and frontline staff. ④ Presenting an evidence gap (organizational and financial integration) as if it were supported raises the cost of later correction [Ev02].
